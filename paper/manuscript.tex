\begin{abstract}
Audio-visual deepfake detection systems that concatenate acoustic and visual features under a fixed weighting scheme are susceptible to performance degradation when only one modality has been manipulated or when a stream is absent at inference time. This paper presents \textbf{RMS-AV}, an audio-visual deepfake detector that combines four mechanisms to address this limitation: (i) a Temporal Saliency Aggregator (TSA) that replaces uniform frame averaging with bidirectional-GRU attention pooling, (ii) a Cross-Modal Forensic Contrastive Incoherence (CM-FCI) module that projects both modalities onto a shared hypersphere and supervises their angular distance with a margin contrastive loss, (iii) a dynamic reliability gate whose softmax weights are conditioned on the CM-FCI incoherence score and whose missing-stream entries are masked to $-10^{4}$ before normalization, and (iv) auxiliary unimodal classification heads that maintain gradient flow to each encoder during joint training. We train and evaluate RMS-AV on a stratified 8{,}000-sample training subset and a 1{,}999-sample held-out test subset of the Localized Audio-Visual DeepFake (LAV-DF) benchmark across four manipulation categories. On clean evaluation, RMS-AV achieves 99.90\% accuracy, 99.87\% Macro-F1, an AUROC of 1.0000, and an expected calibration error of 0.002. Under simulated complete video dropout, the model retains 85.35\% Macro-F1 (a 14.52 percentage-point reduction), and under audio dropout it retains 98.83\% Macro-F1. We report full confusion matrices, per-category accuracy, gate statistics, and calibration figures for all three evaluation conditions, and discuss the significant limitations of our experimental scope, including the reduced training set, the absence of independently trained baselines, and the lack of cross-dataset generalization evidence.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%% ====================================================================

Advances in face synthesis~\cite{rossler2019faceforensics}, lip-sync manipulation, and neural voice cloning have made it possible to produce audiovisual media in which either the visual track, the acoustic track, or both have been replaced with generated content. These forgeries pose practical risks in contexts ranging from identity fraud to misinformation campaigns, and they have motivated a substantial body of detection research.

Early detection systems operate on a single modality. Visual detectors search for spatial artifacts such as blending boundaries, colour inconsistencies, and irregular facial geometry~\cite{rossler2019faceforensics}. Acoustic detectors target spectral traces of vocoders and text-to-speech systems~\cite{korshunov2018deepfakes}. Neither class of detector can identify manipulations that occur exclusively in the other modality.

Multimodal detectors fuse both streams and can, in principle, exploit the physical coupling between lip motion and speech~\cite{haliassos2021lips}. The LAV-DF benchmark~\cite{cai2022lavdf} was constructed specifically to evaluate detection under asymmetric manipulation: its 136{,}304 recordings include samples where only the video is forged, only the audio is forged, both are forged, or neither is forged. Under this asymmetric setting, a detector that assigns equal weight to both modalities may allow an unmanipulated stream to mask forensic cues in the manipulated one. A further practical concern is robustness to missing input: when one stream is absent due to sensor failure or network loss, a fixed-weight concatenation model produces an undefined or degenerate representation.

Several recent studies have addressed adaptive audio-visual gating. Zhou and Lim~\cite{zhou2021joint} proposed a joint audio-visual representation learning framework for deepfake detection. Mittal et al.~\cite{mittal2020emotions} used affective cues to detect inconsistencies between speech emotion and facial expression. Chugh et al.~\cite{chugh2020not} combined phoneme and viseme representations to detect lip-sync deepfakes. Feng et al.~\cite{feng2023selfsuper} explored self-supervised audio-visual contrastive learning for deepfake detection. While these methods incorporate both modalities, they typically assume that both streams are always available and do not provide an explicit mechanism for graceful degradation under stream loss, nor do they separately measure and supervise cross-modal incoherence as an explicit scalar feature available to the gating network.

In this paper, we describe \textbf{RMS-AV} (Reliability- and Manipulation-Aware Selective Audio-Visual Fusion), which integrates four components:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Temporal Saliency Aggregator (TSA):} A bidirectional GRU with learned attention pooling over sampled video frames, designed to weight temporally salient frames (e.g., those near splicing boundaries or lip-sync discontinuities) more heavily than uniform averaging would.
    \item \textbf{Cross-Modal Forensic Contrastive Incoherence (CM-FCI):} A projection head that maps visual and acoustic embeddings onto a unit hypersphere, computes a scalar incoherence score from their cosine distance, and is trained with a margin contrastive loss to separate authentic (concordant) from manipulated (discordant) pairs.
    \item \textbf{Dynamic Reliability Gate with Validity Masking:} A learned gating network whose input includes the CM-FCI incoherence score and whose output weights are masked to suppress absent streams before softmax normalization.
    \item \textbf{Auxiliary Unimodal Heads:} Separate binary classification heads on each encoder's output, trained jointly with the fused prediction to preserve gradient flow to each modality encoder.
\end{enumerate}

We train and evaluate RMS-AV on a stratified subset of the LAV-DF dataset and report results under clean, missing-video, and missing-audio conditions. We emphasise that the present study constitutes a proof-of-concept evaluation on a reduced data scale; we do not claim state-of-the-art performance relative to the full LAV-DF leaderboard, and we identify the experiments that would be needed to substantiate broader claims.

\section{Related Work}
%% ====================================================================

\subsection{Unimodal Deepfake Detection}
Visual deepfake detection has progressed from hand-crafted artefact detectors to convolutional networks~\cite{rossler2019faceforensics} and, more recently, transformer-based architectures~\cite{coccomini2022combining}. Acoustic deepfake detection targets vocoder signatures, phase discontinuities, and spectral anomalies~\cite{korshunov2018deepfakes}. The ASVspoof challenge series~\cite{yamagishi2021asvspoof} has driven progress in audio-only anti-spoofing systems. Neither modality alone can detect forgeries confined to the other channel.

\subsection{Audio-Visual Deepfake Detection}
Multimodal approaches aim to exploit the physiological coupling between speech production and facial motion. Haliassos et al.~\cite{haliassos2021lips} trained a visual-speech model and demonstrated that pre-training on lip reading improves detection generalisation. Zhou and Lim~\cite{zhou2021joint} proposed a joint embedding space for audio-visual deepfake detection. Mittal et al.~\cite{mittal2020emotions} leveraged affective speech-face mismatches. Chugh et al.~\cite{chugh2020not} combined phoneme-viseme features for lip-sync forgery detection. Cai et al.~\cite{cai2022lavdf} introduced the LAV-DF dataset with temporal annotations for localised manipulation detection. Feng et al.~\cite{feng2023selfsuper} proposed a self-supervised contrastive framework for learning audio-visual correspondences without manipulation labels. Ilyas et al.~\cite{ilyas2023avfakenet} developed AVFakeNet, a unified network for joint audio-visual deepfake detection. These methods generally use attention or gating to weight modality contributions, but they do not combine an explicit, supervised cross-modal incoherence metric with a validity-masked gating mechanism for graceful single-stream fallback.

\subsection{Missing-Modality Robustness}
The problem of missing or corrupted modalities has been studied extensively in multimodal sentiment analysis and medical imaging~\cite{ma2022multimodal}. Strategies include modality dropout during training, modality-invariant representations, and reconstruction-based imputation. In deepfake detection, this concern is less explored. Our approach uses input-level validity masking rather than modality dropout augmentation during training---a design choice whose implications we discuss in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

%% ====================================================================

\subsection{Visual Encoder with Temporal Saliency Aggregation}

Given a video, we uniformly sample $T = 8$ frames and resize each to $224 \times 224 \times 3$ with ImageNet normalisation. Each frame $\mathbf{x}_{v,t}$ is processed through a ResNet-18 backbone (initialised from ImageNet-pretrained weights) followed by a linear projection:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{f}_{v,t} = \operatorname{Linear}(\operatorname{ResNet18}(\mathbf{x}_{v,t})) \in \mathbb{R}^D, \quad t \in \{1, \dots, T\}
\end{equation}
where $D = 256$. Standard approaches average these frame embeddings uniformly. We instead apply a \textbf{Temporal Saliency Aggregator (TSA)}: a single-layer bidirectional GRU processes the sequence and the output is combined with the input via a residual connection and layer normalisation:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_{v,t}^{\mathrm{temp}} = \operatorname{BiGRU}(\mathbf{f}_{v,t}), \quad \tilde{\mathbf{f}}_{v,t} = \operatorname{LayerNorm}(\mathbf{f}_{v,t} + \mathbf{h}_{v,t}^{\mathrm{temp}})
\end{equation}
Temporal attention scores are computed by a two-layer MLP with $\tanh$ activation and softmax normalisation:
\begin{equation}
e_t = \mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{sal}}^\top \tanh(\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{sal}} \tilde{\mathbf{f}}_{v,t} + \mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{sal}}), \quad \alpha_t = \frac{\exp(e_t)}{\sum_{k=1}^T \exp(e_k)}
\end{equation}
The visual embedding is the attention-weighted sum:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_v = \sum_{t=1}^T \alpha_t \tilde{\mathbf{f}}_{v,t} \in \mathbb{R}^D
\end{equation}

\subsection{Acoustic Encoder}

Raw audio is resampled to 16\,kHz mono and converted into an 80-band log-mel spectrogram over a 4.0-second window using an STFT with $n_{\mathrm{fft}} = 400$ and hop length 160, followed by amplitude-to-dB conversion and zero-mean unit-variance normalisation per clip. This produces a tensor $\mathbf{X}_a \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times 80 \times 401}$. The spectrogram passes through a four-stage convolutional network (Conv2d$\rightarrow$BN$\rightarrow$ReLU$\rightarrow$MaxPool repeated four times with channel widths $[32, 64, 128, 256]$, ending with adaptive average pooling to $1 \times 1$) and a final linear layer to produce $\mathbf{h}_a \in \mathbb{R}^D$.

\textbf{Audio windowing policy.} During training, when ground-truth temporal annotations (\texttt{fake\_periods}) are available for a given clip, the 4.0-second extraction window is centred on the midpoint of the first annotated manipulation segment. For authentic clips (which have no manipulation annotation), the window starts at the beginning of the file. At test time, the same procedure applies: the evaluation code receives the annotation metadata, and the windowing is applied identically. We acknowledge that this constitutes an oracle windowing strategy that relies on temporal annotation availability. Section~\ref{sec:limitations} discusses the implications of this design.

\subsection{Cross-Modal Forensic Contrastive Incoherence (CM-FCI)}

When both streams are present, we project $\mathbf{h}_v$ and $\mathbf{h}_a$ through separate two-layer MLPs (with batch normalisation and ReLU) into $d_{\mathrm{proj}} = 128$ dimensions and $\ell_2$-normalise onto the unit hypersphere:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{z}_v = \frac{\phi_v(\mathbf{h}_v)}{\|\phi_v(\mathbf{h}_v)\|_2}, \quad \mathbf{z}_a = \frac{\phi_a(\mathbf{h}_a)}{\|\phi_a(\mathbf{h}_a)\|_2}
\end{equation}
The scalar incoherence score is:
\begin{equation}
D_{\mathrm{inc}} = \frac{1 - \mathbf{z}_v^\top \mathbf{z}_a}{2} \in [0, 1]
\end{equation}
This is supervised by a margin contrastive loss:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FCI}} = (1 - y)(1 - \mathbf{z}_v^\top \mathbf{z}_a) + y \max(0, \mathbf{z}_v^\top \mathbf{z}_a - m)
\end{equation}
where $y \in \{0, 1\}$ is the binary manipulation label and $m = 0.20$. For authentic pairs ($y = 0$), the loss encourages cosine similarity towards 1; for manipulated pairs ($y = 1$), it pushes cosine similarity below $m$. When only one stream is present, $D_{\mathrm{inc}}$ is set to 0 and $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FCI}}$ is excluded from the loss.

\subsection{Dynamic Reliability Gate with Validity Masking}

The gating network receives the concatenation $[\mathbf{h}_a; \mathbf{h}_v; D_{\mathrm{inc}}] \in \mathbb{R}^{2D + 1}$ and produces two raw scores through a two-layer MLP:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{s} = \mathbf{W}_2 \operatorname{ReLU}(\mathbf{W}_1 [\mathbf{h}_a; \mathbf{h}_v; D_{\mathrm{inc}}] + \mathbf{b}_1) + \mathbf{b}_2 \in \mathbb{R}^2
\end{equation}
The final layer ($\mathbf{W}_2$, $\mathbf{b}_2$) is initialised to zero so that the initial gate output is $[0.5, 0.5]$.

To handle absent modalities, binary validity indicators $m_a, m_v \in \{0, 1\}$ mask the raw scores before softmax:
\begin{equation}
s_i' = \begin{cases} s_i & \text{if } m_i = 1 \\ -10^4 & \text{if } m_i = 0 \end{cases}, \quad [g_a, g_v] = \operatorname{softmax}([s_a', s_v'])
\end{equation}
Since $\exp(-10^4) \approx 0$, a missing stream receives gate weight $g_i \approx 0$, and the remaining stream receives weight $\approx 1$. The value $-10^4$ is chosen to remain within the representable range of IEEE 754 half-precision arithmetic (FP16), avoiding overflow in the $\exp(\cdot)$ computation during mixed-precision training.

\textbf{Role of masking during clean evaluation.} When both modalities are present ($m_a = m_v = 1$), the masking operation is an identity: $s_i' = s_i$. The mask therefore does not affect the model's behaviour on clean data. We clarify this point because it was a source of confusion in our early experiments.

\subsection{Selective Fusion and Multi-Task Training Objective}

The gated representation is:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{fused}} = [g_v \mathbf{h}_v; \; g_a \mathbf{h}_a] \in \mathbb{R}^{2D}
\end{equation}
which is classified by a two-layer MLP (Linear$\rightarrow$ReLU$\rightarrow$Dropout(0.3)$\rightarrow$Linear) producing a single logit $\hat{y}_{\mathrm{av}}$. The total training loss is:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE}}(\hat{y}_{\mathrm{av}}, y) + \lambda_{\mathrm{aux}}(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE}}(\hat{y}_a, y_a) + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE}}(\hat{y}_v, y_v)) + \lambda_{\mathrm{FCI}} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FCI}}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{y}_a$ and $\hat{y}_v$ are unimodal logits from auxiliary two-layer MLP heads, $y_a$ and $y_v$ are modality-specific manipulation labels, $\lambda_{\mathrm{aux}} = 0.20$, and $\lambda_{\mathrm{FCI}} = 0.15$.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{RMS-AV Inference}
\label{alg:rmsav}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Video frames $\{\mathbf{x}_{v,t}\}_{t=1}^T$, audio $\mathbf{x}_a$, validity flags $m_v, m_a \in \{0,1\}$
\ENSURE Deepfake probability $\hat{p}$, gate weights $[g_v, g_a]$, incoherence $D_{\mathrm{inc}}$
\STATE $\mathbf{f}_{v,t} \leftarrow \operatorname{Linear}(\operatorname{ResNet18}(\mathbf{x}_{v,t}))$ for $t = 1, \dots, T$
\STATE $\mathbf{h}_v, \{\alpha_t\} \leftarrow \operatorname{TSA}(\{\mathbf{f}_{v,t}\})$
\STATE $\mathbf{h}_a \leftarrow f_a(\mathbf{x}_a)$
\IF{$m_a = 1$ \AND $m_v = 1$}
    \STATE $\mathbf{z}_v \leftarrow \phi_v(\mathbf{h}_v) / \|\phi_v(\mathbf{h}_v)\|_2$; $\mathbf{z}_a \leftarrow \phi_a(\mathbf{h}_a) / \|\phi_a(\mathbf{h}_a)\|_2$
    \STATE $D_{\mathrm{inc}} \leftarrow (1 - \mathbf{z}_v^\top \mathbf{z}_a)/2$
\ELSE
    \STATE $D_{\mathrm{inc}} \leftarrow 0$
\ENDIF
\STATE $\mathbf{s} \leftarrow \operatorname{GateNet}([\mathbf{h}_a; \mathbf{h}_v; D_{\mathrm{inc}}])$
\STATE Mask: $s_i' \leftarrow s_i$ if $m_i = 1$, else $s_i' \leftarrow -10^4$
\STATE $[g_a, g_v] \leftarrow \operatorname{softmax}([s_a', s_v'])$
\STATE $\hat{y}_{\mathrm{av}} \leftarrow \operatorname{JointHead}([g_v \mathbf{h}_v; g_a \mathbf{h}_a])$
\STATE $\hat{p} \leftarrow \sigma(\hat{y}_{\mathrm{av}})$
\RETURN $\hat{p}, [g_v, g_a], D_{\mathrm{inc}}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Experimental Setup}
%% ====================================================================

\subsection{Dataset}

We use the Localized Audio-Visual DeepFake (LAV-DF) dataset~\cite{cai2022lavdf}, which contains 136{,}304 media recordings distributed across official train, validation (dev), and test splits. Each recording is annotated with binary flags indicating whether the video track, the audio track, or both have been manipulated, together with temporal boundaries of the manipulation segments.

The full dataset partitions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Train:} 78{,}703 clips (21{,}254 real, 57{,}449 fake).
    \item \textbf{Dev (validation):} 31{,}501 clips (8{,}271 real, 23{,}230 fake).
    \item \textbf{Test:} 26{,}100 clips (6{,}906 real, 19{,}194 fake).
\end{itemize}
All manipulated clips derived from a given source recording reside in the same partition as the source, preventing identity leakage across splits~\cite{cai2022lavdf}.

\textbf{Subset selection.} Due to computational constraints (single Kaggle session with two NVIDIA T4 GPUs, 16\,GB VRAM each), we train on a deterministic stratified random sample of 8{,}000 clips from the official training partition and evaluate on a stratified sample of 1{,}999 clips from the official test partition. Stratification is performed with a fixed random seed (42) across four categories: real, audio-only fake, video-only fake, and both-modalities fake, maintaining approximately proportional representation. This subset evaluation is a significant limitation discussed in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsection{Implementation Details}

RMS-AV is implemented in PyTorch 2.x. The visual backbone is ResNet-18 initialised with ImageNet-pretrained weights. Training uses the AdamW optimiser with learning rate $10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$, batch size 32, and cosine annealing to a minimum learning rate of $10^{-6}$ over 5 epochs. Automatic mixed precision (AMP) is used with an initial gradient scaler of 1024 and gradient norm clipping at 5.0. The best checkpoint is selected by validation Macro-F1. Training augmentations include random horizontal flips, slight brightness/contrast jitter on video frames, and SpecAugment (frequency masking with parameter 15, time masking with parameter 35) on audio spectrograms.

\textbf{Important note on modality dropout.} The model is trained with \emph{both modalities always present}. No modality dropout augmentation is applied during training. The missing-modality robustness results reported in Section~\ref{sec:results} therefore reflect the model's zero-shot generalisation to stream absence, not a trained capability. This is a deliberate design choice to isolate the contribution of the architectural masking mechanism, but it means that training with modality dropout could potentially improve the missing-modality results further.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}

We evaluate the trained RMS-AV checkpoint under three conditions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Clean:} Both audio and video streams provided as-is.
    \item \textbf{Missing video:} Video tensor zeroed, validity flag $m_v = 0$.
    \item \textbf{Missing audio:} Audio tensor zeroed, validity flag $m_a = 0$.
\end{enumerate}

For each condition, we report: accuracy, balanced accuracy, precision, recall, class-specific F1 scores (F1\textsubscript{fake}, F1\textsubscript{real}), Macro-F1 (arithmetic mean of F1\textsubscript{fake} and F1\textsubscript{real}), AUROC (computed from continuous predicted probabilities), PR-AUC, Brier score, expected calibration error (ECE), per-category accuracy, and the raw confusion matrix (TP, TN, FP, FN).

ECE is computed using 10 equal-width bins over the predicted probability range $[0, 1]$:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{ECE} = \sum_{b=1}^{10} \frac{|B_b|}{N} \left| \mathrm{acc}(B_b) - \mathrm{conf}(B_b) \right|
\end{equation}
where $B_b$ is the set of samples whose predicted probability falls in bin $b$, $\mathrm{acc}(B_b)$ is the observed accuracy in that bin, and $\mathrm{conf}(B_b)$ is the mean predicted probability in that bin. All calibration is computed on the held-out test subset; no post-hoc calibration (e.g., temperature scaling) is applied.

The key observations are:

\textbf{Missing video} reduces Macro-F1 from 99.87\% to 85.35\% (a 14.52 percentage-point drop). The 249 false negatives are concentrated in the video-only fake category, which drops from 100\% to 58.50\% accuracy. This is expected: when the video stream is absent, the model relies entirely on audio, and video-only forgeries leave no trace in the audio channel. The audio-only fake and both-modalities fake categories retain above 95\% accuracy because they contain audio manipulation that the acoustic encoder can detect.

\textbf{Missing audio} produces a much smaller degradation: Macro-F1 drops from 99.87\% to 98.83\% (1.04 percentage points). There are 18 additional false positives but zero false negatives. The visual encoder alone is sufficient to detect all manipulated categories, and the only errors are a small number of authentic clips incorrectly flagged. This asymmetry in degradation---the visual encoder is substantially more self-sufficient than the acoustic encoder---is consistent with the gate's learned preference for the visual modality ($g_v = 0.85$) during clean evaluation.

\textbf{Calibration degradation.} The ECE increases from 0.002 (clean) to 0.128 (missing video), indicating that the model's probability estimates become poorly calibrated when operating with only audio. This is expected since the model was never trained with missing modalities, and suggests that post-hoc calibration or modality dropout training would be needed for deployment with unreliable streams.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%% ====================================================================

\subsection{Interpretation of Near-Perfect Clean Results}

The clean evaluation results (99.90\% accuracy, 99.87\% Macro-F1, AUROC of 1.0000) appear unusually strong. Several factors likely contribute to this performance level, and we discuss them critically.

\textbf{Oracle audio windowing.} The audio preprocessing centres the 4.0-second extraction window on the annotated manipulation midpoint. This means the model is guaranteed to receive the manipulated audio segment when one exists. At inference on unseen data without temporal annotations, an alternative strategy (e.g., sliding window, voice activity detection, or processing the full audio) would be needed, and performance may differ.

\textbf{Subset evaluation.} The 1{,}999-sample test subset is drawn by stratified random sampling from the 26{,}100-sample official test partition. Although stratification preserves category proportions, the reduced sample size means the evaluation does not reflect the full diversity of the LAV-DF test set. Two errors out of 1{,}999 samples corresponds to an error rate of 0.10\%, but the 95\% Clopper-Pearson confidence interval for this error rate is approximately [0.01\%, 0.36\%], which is wide relative to the point estimate.

\textbf{Transfer learning from ImageNet.} The ResNet-18 backbone is initialised with ImageNet-pretrained weights, which provides strong low-level visual feature extraction. Combined with a relatively small training set (8{,}000 clips), the pretrained features may account for much of the visual detection capability, and the TSA and gating mechanisms may contribute less marginal improvement than they would on a randomly initialised backbone. We cannot disentangle these contributions without running ablation experiments on the same data with the same training budget.

\subsection{Missing-Modality Analysis}

The missing-modality results demonstrate that the architectural validity masking provides a functional fallback mechanism even without explicit training for stream absence. However, two points merit emphasis:

First, the missing-video degradation is entirely concentrated in the video-only fake category (58.50\% accuracy). This is not a model failure---it is a fundamental limitation: video-only forgeries leave no detectable trace in the audio channel. No amount of architectural design can overcome the absence of the relevant signal.

Second, no modality dropout was applied during training. The baselines against which missing-modality robustness would ideally be compared---models trained with equivalent modality dropout---were not implemented due to computational constraints. It is therefore unknown whether the observed robustness is primarily due to the validity masking mechanism or would also be achieved by a simple concatenation model trained with dropout.

\subsection{Absence of Independently Trained Baselines}

The present study evaluates only the full RMS-AV model. We do not report results for audio-only, video-only, or standard concatenation fusion baselines trained on the same 8{,}000-sample subset. Without these comparisons, we cannot claim that the TSA, CM-FCI, or gating mechanisms improve detection performance relative to simpler alternatives. The numbers in Tables~\ref{tab:clean_results}--\ref{tab:stress_results} describe the absolute performance of a single architectural configuration, not a comparative evaluation.

\subsection{CM-FCI Incoherence Separation}

The CM-FCI module produces a clear empirical separation between authentic and manipulated samples on the validation set: $D_{\mathrm{inc}}^{\mathrm{real}} = 0.007$ versus $D_{\mathrm{inc}}^{\mathrm{fake}} = 0.666$ at Epoch 4 (Table~\ref{tab:training_history}). This $95\times$ ratio suggests that the contrastive objective successfully shapes the projection space, but we note two caveats: (i) the incoherence metric has not been evaluated as a standalone binary classifier (e.g., by thresholding $D_{\mathrm{inc}}$ directly and reporting its detection accuracy), and (ii) the contribution of $D_{\mathrm{inc}}$ as a feature to the gating network, versus the raw embeddings $\mathbf{h}_a$ and $\mathbf{h}_v$ that the gate also receives, has not been isolated through ablation.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}
%% ====================================================================

We identify the following limitations and corresponding directions for future work:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Reduced training and evaluation scale.} The model is trained on 8{,}000 clips and evaluated on 1{,}999 clips, compared to the full LAV-DF benchmark of 78{,}703 training and 26{,}100 test clips. Full-scale training and evaluation are necessary before performance claims can be contextualised against published results on this benchmark.

    \item \textbf{No independently trained baselines.} Comparative evaluation against audio-only, video-only, standard concatenation, and ablated (no-TSA, no-FCI, no-gate) variants trained on the identical subset is required to quantify the contribution of each component.

    \item \textbf{Oracle audio windowing.} The manipulation-aware audio cropping policy uses ground-truth temporal annotations at both training and test time. For practical deployment, this must be replaced by an annotation-free strategy (e.g., processing the full audio, voice activity detection, or a learned temporal attention mechanism applied to the raw waveform).

    \item \textbf{No modality dropout training.} The model is trained with both streams always present. Comparing against a version trained with stochastic modality dropout (e.g., randomly zeroing one stream with probability $p$) would clarify whether the missing-modality robustness is architectural or could be achieved more simply.

    \item \textbf{No cross-dataset evaluation.} All training and evaluation use LAV-DF. Generalization to other datasets (e.g., FakeAVCeleb~\cite{khalid2021fakeavceleb}, DFDC~\cite{dolhansky2020dfdc}, or WildDeepfake) and to manipulation methods not represented in LAV-DF has not been tested.

    \item \textbf{Single training run.} Results are from a single random seed (42). Reporting mean and standard deviation across multiple seeds would provide uncertainty estimates. The 95\% confidence interval on the clean error rate (2/1{,}999) is approximately [0.01\%, 0.36\%].

    \item \textbf{Calibration under distribution shift.} The ECE of 0.128 under missing-video conditions indicates that predicted probabilities become unreliable when the input distribution shifts from training conditions. Post-hoc calibration methods (e.g., temperature scaling on a validation set) should be explored.

    \item \textbf{Temporal localisation not evaluated.} LAV-DF provides temporal boundaries for manipulation segments, and the original benchmark includes temporal localisation metrics. Our model performs clip-level binary classification only and does not attempt temporal localisation.
\end{enumerate}

%% ====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
%% ====================================================================

We presented RMS-AV, an audio-visual deepfake detection architecture that integrates temporal saliency aggregation over video frames, a supervised cross-modal contrastive incoherence metric, a dynamic reliability gate with validity masking for missing-stream handling, and auxiliary unimodal classification heads. Evaluated on a stratified subset of the LAV-DF benchmark (8{,}000 training clips, 1{,}999 test clips), RMS-AV achieves 99.90\% accuracy and 99.87\% Macro-F1 on clean evaluation, retains 85.35\% Macro-F1 under complete video dropout (with degradation concentrated in the expected category of video-only forgeries), and retains 98.83\% Macro-F1 under audio dropout. These results demonstrate functional missing-modality fallback through architectural validity masking, even without modality dropout training.

We have been explicit about the limitations of the current evaluation: the reduced data scale, the absence of trained baselines and ablation experiments, the oracle audio windowing policy, and the lack of cross-dataset evidence. These limitations define the scope of the present contribution as a proof-of-concept architectural study rather than a state-of-the-art benchmark result. Future work will address full-scale training, comparative and ablation evaluation, annotation-free audio processing, and cross-dataset generalisation.
